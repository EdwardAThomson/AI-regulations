# UAE National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence 2031

*Research and comparison only; not legal advice. AI-assisted analysis that may contain errors; verify against the primary source.*

**Document:** UAE National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence 2031 (National Program for Artificial Intelligence)
**Launched:** October 2017 (this version dated 2018)
**Issuing body:** UAE Minister of State for Artificial Intelligence / AI Office
**Legal status:** National strategy and vision document; not binding regulation
**Length:** ~25 pages

---

## Summary

The UAE National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence 2031 is the country's flagship AI strategy, the world's first national AI strategy and the first published under a dedicated Minister of State for Artificial Intelligence. It sets an ambition-led vision: to make the UAE "one of the leading nations in AI by 2031," in alignment with the UAE Centennial 2071, and to shift the country from an adopter of AI to a builder and exporter of it. The document is structured around eight strategic objectives, supported by economic projections and a set of delivery vehicles, but it is a roadmap of intentions rather than a binding or operational framework.

## Key points

- **Vision and framing:** Become one of the leading nations in AI by 2031; tied to the UAE Centennial 2071; positioned as making the UAE "the world's most prepared country for artificial intelligence." A pyramid model arranges the work into Vision, Leadership, AI Activity, and Foundation layers.
- **Eight strategic objectives:** (1) build a reputation as an AI destination; (2) increase UAE competitive assets in priority sectors through deployment of AI; (3) develop a fertile ecosystem for AI; (4) adopt AI across customer services to improve lives and government; (5) attract and train talent for future jobs enabled by AI; (6) bring world-leading research capability to work with target industries; (7) provide the data and supporting infrastructure essential to become a test bed for AI; (8) ensure strong governance and effective regulation.
- **Priority sectors (five):** Resources & Energy, Logistics & Transport, Tourism & Hospitality, Healthcare, and Cybersecurity. The document notes priorities may change as the economy matures.
- **Economic projections:** AI could generate up to AED 335 billion in increased economic output (described as a 26% increase using national statistics); a cited PwC estimate puts AI's contribution at AED 353 billion to GDP by 2030 (13.6% of GDP); IDC's MEA AI-spending estimate is cited as reaching AED 419.54 million by 2021, growing 32% a year.
- **Delivery mechanisms:** the AI Office (Office of the Minister of State for AI); the UAE Artificial Intelligence and Blockchain Council, which is to oversee implementation across emirates; a "UAI" brand and a four-level UAI Seal of Approval (Public Sector, Private Sector, Institutional, Product); an Applied AI Accelerator; an AI Network of researchers and policy experts; National AI Challenges; a National Virtual AI Institute; a Key Thinkers Program; an open-access AI Library in English and Arabic; and the Mohammed bin Rashid Innovation Fund (cited at AED 2 billion).
- **Talent targets:** upskilling one-third of the UAE's STEM graduates per year (stated as ~2,000 students) toward world-class AI talent; over 5,000 UAE residents trained on AI fundamentals; advanced government courses starting Q4 2018; an aim that 100% of senior government leadership be trained and versed in AI.
- **Data and infrastructure:** an ambition to create an open, standardised, AI-ready data-sharing program and a secure data infrastructure; notes the UAE had 537 open datasets at the time, lagging comparators (Turkey 1,280; Canada over 10,000); cites Estonia's X-Road and Australia's SURE as models.
- **Governance commitments (Objective 8):** a National Governance Review by the AI and Blockchain Council covering data management, ethics and cybersecurity; hosting a Second Global Governance of AI Roundtable at the 2019 World Government Summit (with UNESCO, OECD, IEEE); and an ambition to help create an intergovernmental panel on AI (an "IPCC for AI"), working with France and others. References to GDPR and "ethics-by-design" training appear, but no binding rules are set out.

## Observations

### Measurability

The strategy is heavy on aspiration and light on measurable success criteria. The headline outcome ("one of the leading nations in AI by 2031") has no defined metric, baseline, or ranking against which success or failure could be judged. Most economic figures are potential or third-party projections (PwC, IDC, Forrester), not UAE targets with accountability attached, and several are presented with little methodological transparency; the AED 419.54 million IDC figure in particular reads as anomalously small against the billions cited elsewhere and should be treated cautiously. The clearest quantified commitments sit in the talent strand: upskilling roughly 2,000 STEM graduates a year, training over 5,000 residents, training 100% of senior government leadership, and starting government courses in Q4 2018. These are concrete and trackable, but they are inputs (people trained) rather than outcomes (capability built or value delivered), and most other objectives lack even input-level metrics.

### Gaps

For a document whose eighth objective is "ensure strong governance and effective regulation," the regulatory content is notably thin. There is no risk-classification scheme, no list of prohibited or high-risk practices, no enforcement mechanism, no liability or redress regime, and no binding obligations on developers or deployers. Governance is treated as something to be reviewed, debated, and convened internationally rather than codified domestically. Safety, ethics, bias, transparency, and human oversight are gestured at (a "values-driven approach," "ethics-by-design training," recognising "unintentional consequences"), but none is developed into a standard or requirement. Data protection is acknowledged only by pointing to Europe's GDPR as an external reference, with the consultative group under Objective 8 left to "begin to address these issues" later. The strategy is best read as the opening move of a soft-law, pro-innovation posture: the binding and voluntary instruments came later (the UAE's voluntary AI Charter in 2024 and subsequent sector guidance), not here.

### Coverage

Coverage is broad in ambition but shallow in operational depth, and it is firmly vision-led. The document spans branding, sector deployment, ecosystem and startups, government services, talent, research, data infrastructure, and governance, which is a wide canvas for a single strategy. But each strand is described at the level of intent and illustrative example (frequently borrowing other countries' programs as templates: Singapore's Smart Nation, Estonia's X-Road, Australia's SURE and National Missions Program, the UK's Alan Turing Institute) rather than through detailed UAE implementation plans, budgets, owners, or timelines. The scope is the AI of its era: machine learning, automation, and data-driven services. It predates and therefore does not address foundation models, generative AI, or agentic systems, which is expected for a 2017 document but limits its relevance to current AI risk debates.

### Internal consistency

The eight objectives form a reasonably coherent ladder, and the strategy is candid that Objective 1 (reputation) depends on delivering the other seven. The main internal tension is between the document's stated emphasis on "strong governance and effective regulation" and its near-total absence of concrete governance instruments: the ambition is asserted but not operationalised within the text. There is also a mild tension between positioning the UAE as both a fast adopter using imported AI and a future builder and exporter of AI, with limited detail on how the transition between those roles is financed or sequenced beyond the accelerator and innovation-fund mentions.

### Feasibility

The state-led, top-down model is well suited to the UAE's governance structure: a centralised AI Office, a dedicated minister, an empowered council, and a government that is itself a major employer and AI customer give the strategy real levers, particularly for the government-adoption and talent objectives, which look achievable. The more demanding objectives (becoming a global AI destination, hosting world-leading research, building an indigenous build-and-export AI industry) face the same structural constraints the document itself concedes: a thin research base (the strategy reports the UAE at roughly 0.19% of population in full-time STIE research, far below leaders), reliance on attracting foreign talent and firms, and competition from established hubs. These are aspirational stretch goals rather than near-term deliverables, and the absence of milestones makes progress hard to verify.

### International alignment

The strategy is outward-looking and benchmarks itself heavily against international peers, repeatedly citing US, UK, China, France, Singapore, Estonia, Australia, and Saudi Arabia initiatives as models. On governance it aligns with the convening, multilateral end of the spectrum: hosting global roundtables with UNESCO, OECD, and IEEE, and backing a France-linked "IPCC for AI." This places the UAE alongside soft-law, principles-and-dialogue approaches rather than the binding, risk-tiered model the EU later adopted in the AI Act. As a first-mover national strategy it influenced the wave of national AI strategies that followed, but its governance philosophy is one of participation in standard-setting rather than domestic rule-making.

## Overall assessment

The UAE National Strategy for AI 2031 is a confident, first-of-its-kind vision document that succeeds as a statement of ambition and as a signal of high-level political commitment, anchored by the world's first AI minister and a clear tie to the UAE Centennial 2071. Its strengths are breadth, a coherent objective structure, and a credible state-as-adopter delivery model with concrete talent and government-training commitments. Its weaknesses are the flip side of its nature: it is not regulation and does not pretend to be one in substance, so it offers no risk framework, no safety or ethics standards, no enforcement, and few measurable outcome targets outside the talent strand. Read correctly, it is the foundational vision layer of the UAE's pro-innovation, soft-law approach, with the operational and (voluntary) governance detail intended to follow through later instruments rather than to be found in this document.
