# UAE Charter for the Development and Use of Artificial Intelligence

*Research and comparison only, not legal advice. AI-assisted analysis; may contain errors, so verify all claims against the primary source document.*

**Document:** The UAE Charter for the Development & Use of Artificial Intelligence
**Published:** July 2024 (announced June 2024)
**Issuing body:** UAE Minister of State for Artificial Intelligence, Digital Economy & Remote Work Applications Office
**Legal status:** Voluntary, non-binding ethics charter; no enforcement mechanism, no penalties, no defined compliance process
**Length:** ~6 pages

---

## Summary

The UAE Charter for the Development and Use of Artificial Intelligence is a short, principles-based ethics statement issued in 2024 by the UAE's Minister of State for AI, Digital Economy and Remote Work Applications Office. It sets out twelve high-level principles intended to "protect the rights of the UAE community" in the development and use of AI, and it is positioned as a guiding framework aligned with the UAE Strategy for Artificial Intelligence, which targets AI leadership by 2031. It is aspirational rather than regulatory: the document creates no legal obligations and describes no mechanism for monitoring, compliance, or redress.

## Key points

The Charter opens with an Introduction framing the UAE's ambition to be "a global leader in the ethical oversight and use of artificial intelligence," and a note on its relation to the UAE AI Strategy. It then lists twelve "General Principles":

1. **Strengthening Human-Machine Ties**: enhance a harmonious, beneficial relationship between AI and humans, with all AI development prioritising human well-being and progress.
2. **Safety**: AI systems should comply with the "highest safety standards"; the UAE encourages modifying or removing systems that pose risks.
3. **Fairness**: address algorithmic bias to deliver a fair and equitable environment, inclusive and accessible AI, support for diversity, and equal technological benefits without exclusion or discrimination.
4. **Data Privacy**: while data is essential to AI, the privacy of community members remains a top priority, in line with the UAE's stance on privacy rights.
5. **Transparency**: create a clear understanding of how AI systems operate and make decisions, to build trust, responsibility, and accountability.
6. **Human Oversight**: affirm the "irreplaceable value of human judgment and human oversight" over AI to correct errors or biases, aligned with ethical values and social standards.
7. **Governance and Accountability**: adopt a responsible, proactive stance emphasising governance and accountability so AI is used ethically and transparently.
8. **Technological Excellence**: treat AI as a "beacon of innovation," pursuing global leadership, competitiveness, and quality-of-life improvements through technological and scientific excellence.
9. **Human Commitment**: place human values and fundamental rights at the heart of innovation to ensure AI serves the public good with lasting positive impact.
10. **Peaceful Coexistence with AI**: ensure AI enhances community well-being and progress without compromising human security or fundamental rights.
11. **Promoting AI Awareness for an Inclusive Future**: build an inclusive future with equitable access to AI and its benefits for all segments of society.
12. **Commitment to Treaties and Applicable Laws**: comply with international treaties and local laws in the development and use of AI.

Notably, the document contains no definitions section, no scope clause specifying who it applies to, no risk classification, no named oversight body, and no implementation timeline.

## Observations

### Legal status

This is the single most important interpretive point. The Charter is voluntary and non-binding. It calls itself a "guiding framework" and uses aspirational verbs throughout ("aims to," "seeks to," "emphasizes," "encourages"). There is no statutory basis cited, no obligation imposed on any party, no penalty for non-adherence, no licensing or registration requirement, and no compliance mechanism of any kind. It should not be read as regulation or as a source of enforceable duties. It sits firmly within the UAE's soft-law, pro-innovation posture: a signal of intent and a reputational marker rather than a rulebook. Any organisation operating in the UAE remains governed by actual binding instruments (for example federal data protection law and sector regulation), none of which this Charter modifies.

### Measurability

The principles are stated as values, not as targets. None carries a metric, baseline, threshold, reporting requirement, or success criterion. Phrases such as "highest safety standards" (Principle 2) and "clear understanding" of AI decision-making (Principle 5) are undefined and therefore not testable. There is no accountability loop: nobody is designated to assess whether a principle has been met, and there is no cadence of review. Because adherence is self-declared and unmeasured, the Charter could be cited as satisfied by almost any actor without demonstrable change in behaviour. This is characteristic of an ethics declaration rather than a governance framework.

### Clarity

At the level of theme the document is clear and readable, and the twelve headings are easy to follow. At the level of operational meaning it is vague. Several principles overlap and are hard to distinguish: Principle 1 (Human-Machine Ties), Principle 6 (Human Oversight), Principle 9 (Human Commitment), and Principle 10 (Peaceful Coexistence) all restate variations of "keep humans and human values central," without delineating distinct obligations. Principle 7 (Governance and Accountability) names the concepts of governance and accountability but does not say who governs, who is accountable, or to whom. The absence of definitions (including a working definition of "AI") leaves scope and application open.

### Gaps

The most significant gaps for a governance instrument:

- **No risk tiers.** Unlike the EU AI Act's prohibited / high-risk / limited-risk structure, the Charter applies the same flat set of values to all AI with no differentiation by use case or potential harm.
- **No oversight body.** No regulator, authority, ombudsman, or audit function is named to interpret, monitor, or enforce the principles.
- **No remedy for individuals.** Despite framing itself as protecting "the rights of the UAE community," it grants no individual rights, no complaint channel, no right to explanation, no right to contest automated decisions, and no redress for harm.
- **No prohibitions.** No AI practice is banned; Principle 2 only "encourages" modifying or removing risky systems.
- **No GPAI or frontier-model provisions, no incident reporting, no transparency-to-consumer obligation, and no implementation roadmap.**

### Feasibility

Because nothing in the Charter is mandatory, it is trivially "feasible" to publish and to claim alignment with. The harder question is whether it advances the UAE's stated goal of ethical AI leadership, and here the lack of any delivery mechanism is the limiting factor. The values are reasonable and broadly shared internationally, but without instruments to operationalise them (standards, guidance, audits, or binding rules) the Charter depends entirely on voluntary uptake and on future legislation that it does not itself create.

### Internal consistency

The principles do not contradict one another, and they are coherent with the Charter's framing and with the UAE AI Strategy 2031 it references. The main internal tension is one of redundancy rather than conflict: the four human-centred principles cover heavily overlapping ground, which dilutes the document and suggests breadth was prioritised over precision. The recurring "innovation and competitiveness" emphasis (Principles 8 and 11) coexists comfortably with the protective principles only because none of them is binding; a real regulatory text would have to resolve where, for example, safety constraints limit deployment speed.

### Coverage

Thematically the twelve principles touch most of the recognised pillars of AI ethics: human oversight, safety, fairness and bias, privacy, transparency, accountability, inclusion, and legal compliance. As a checklist of values the coverage is reasonably complete. As coverage of the AI governance landscape it is thin: it does not address foundation or general-purpose models, agentic systems, sectoral applications, national security uses, liability allocation, or enforcement, and it offers no scope statement defining covered actors or systems.

### International alignment

The Charter's values map closely onto the established international canon, which is unsurprising given how widely these themes are now shared:

- **OECD AI Principles (2019, updated 2024):** strong overlap. OECD's human-centred values, transparency and explainability, robustness and safety, and accountability correspond to the Charter's Principles 1, 5, 2, and 7. The Charter, however, omits the OECD's "inclusive growth, sustainable development and well-being" framing as an operational commitment and, more importantly, lacks the OECD's accompanying implementation guidance.
- **UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021):** thematic alignment on human oversight, fairness, privacy, and awareness/education (the Charter's Principle 11 echoes UNESCO's emphasis on AI literacy). UNESCO, though, pairs its values with concrete policy action areas and a readiness-assessment methodology that the Charter has no equivalent of.
- **EU AI Act (2024):** alignment is thematic only. The Charter shares the EU's concerns about safety, transparency, bias, and human oversight, but diverges sharply in form: the EU instrument is binding, risk-tiered, with prohibited practices, conformity assessment, market surveillance, and substantial penalties, whereas the Charter is voluntary and unstructured. The two are not comparable as legal instruments.

In short, the Charter is well aligned at the level of values with OECD, UNESCO, and the thematic concerns of the EU AI Act, but it sits at the soft-law end of the spectrum, closer to a national restatement of OECD-style principles than to a regulatory regime.

## Overall assessment

The UAE Charter for the Development and Use of AI is a concise, values-based ethics declaration that credibly signals the UAE's intent to be seen as a responsible AI nation and aligns neatly with the country's 2031 AI ambitions. Its twelve principles are sensible and consistent with the OECD and UNESCO mainstream, and as a statement of direction it does its job. As a governance instrument, however, it is deliberately light: it is voluntary and non-binding, defines no metrics, names no oversight body, creates no individual rights or remedies, and contains no risk tiers, prohibitions, or enforcement. It is best understood as soft law within a pro-innovation strategy, a marker of values that will only translate into protection if and when the UAE pairs it with binding standards, guidance, and law. Readers should not mistake adherence to the Charter for compliance with any actual regulatory obligation.
