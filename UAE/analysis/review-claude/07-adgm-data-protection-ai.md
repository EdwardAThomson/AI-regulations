# ADGM Data Protection Regulations 2021: AI and Automated Decision-Making Provisions

*Research and comparison only; not legal advice. AI-assisted analysis that may contain errors; verify every claim against the primary source.*

**Document:** ADGM Data Protection Regulations 2021 (Consolidated Version, reflecting amendments through 26 July 2022)
**Issuing body:** Board of Directors of the Abu Dhabi Global Market, under Article 6(1) of Abu Dhabi Law No. 4 of 2013; enforced by the Commissioner of Data Protection / Office of Data Protection
**Legal status:** Binding regulations within the ADGM (an independent common-law financial free zone). GDPR-modelled data-protection law, NOT an AI-specific regime
**In force:** Enacted 11 February 2021; applicable in the ADGM (section 64). Subsequently amended (e.g. 26 July 2022)
**Scope:** Controllers and Processors that Process Personal Data wholly or partly by automated means within, or controlled from, the ADGM

---

## Summary

The ADGM Data Protection Regulations 2021 are a comprehensive, GDPR-modelled data-protection statute for the Abu Dhabi Global Market. They are not an AI law and contain no AI-specific machinery, but they reach AI indirectly through one provision: Section 20, the ADGM analogue of GDPR Article 22, which gives a data subject the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated Processing (including Profiling) that produces legal or similarly significant effects. The relevant AI footprint is therefore narrow and conventional: a right against certain solely-automated decisions, a set of exceptions and safeguards, a Profiling definition, transparency duties to disclose automated decision-making and the logic involved, and a general data-protection-impact-assessment requirement for high-risk Processing. Critically, the ADGM has no equivalent of DIFC Regulation 10: no concept of high-risk AI, no AI register, no certification regime, and no Autonomous Systems Officer.

---

## Key points

- **The core right (Section 20(1)).** A data subject "has the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated Processing, including Profiling, which produces legal effects concerning him or her, or similarly significantly affects him or her." This is the operative AI-relevant provision and is a near-verbatim transposition of GDPR Article 22(1).
- **Three exceptions (Section 20(2)).** The Section 20(1) right does not apply where the decision is: (a) necessary for entering into or performing a contract between the data subject and a Controller; (b) based on the data subject's explicit Consent; or (c) required or authorised by Applicable Law, expressly "including for fraud prevention, anti-money laundering and security and integrity purposes." The Applicable-Law route in 20(2)(c) carries its own conditions: the Controller must notify the data subject in writing as soon as reasonably practicable that a solely-automated decision was taken, and the data subject then has one month to request reconsideration or a non-solely-automated decision.
- **Safeguards (Section 20(3)).** In the contract and explicit-consent cases (20(2)(a) and (b)), the Controller must implement "suitable measures to safeguard the Data Subject's rights and legitimate interests, at least the right to obtain human intervention on the part of the Controller, to express his or her point of view and to contest the decision."
- **Special-category-data restriction (Section 20(4)).** Decisions falling under Section 20(2) "must not be based on Special Categories of Personal Data" unless an exception in Section 7(2)(a) or 7(2)(k) applies and suitable safeguarding measures are in place. This mirrors GDPR Article 22(4).
- **Profiling definition (Section 62).** "Profiling" means "any form of automated Processing of Personal Data consisting of the use of Personal Data to evaluate certain personal aspects relating to a natural person," in particular to analyse or predict performance at work, economic situation, health, personal preferences, interests, reliability, behaviour, location or movements. This is the GDPR Article 4(4) definition.
- **Transparency duty to disclose the logic.** The information-notice provisions (the Article 13/14-equivalent sections at 11, 12 and 13) each require the Controller to disclose "the existence of automated decision-making, including Profiling, referred to in sections 20(1) and 20(4) and, at least in those cases, meaningful information about the logic involved, as well as the significance and the envisaged consequences of such Processing for the Data Subject."
- **Data Protection Impact Assessment (Section 34).** Before Processing "likely to result in a high risk to the rights of natural persons," the Controller must carry out a DPIA describing the nature, scope, context and purpose of the Processing, assessing necessity, proportionality and compliance, identifying and assessing risks to individuals, and identifying mitigations (34(5)). The Commissioner publishes a list of Processing operations that require a DPIA (34(4)), and prior-consultation with the Commissioner is required where a DPIA indicates residual high risk (34(7)). This is the GDPR Article 35 analogue and is the closest the Regulations come to a structured AI risk assessment, but it is generic and not AI-specific.

## Observations

### Legal status

This is the analytically decisive point, and it cuts the opposite way from DIFC Regulation 10. The ADGM Data Protection Regulations 2021 are genuinely binding subordinate legislation enacted by the ADGM Board, carrying the force of the Commissioner's enforcement powers, administrative fines and the data-subject remedies in Parts VII and VIII. But they are binding data-protection law, not binding AI law. Nothing in the instrument regulates AI, autonomous systems, or machine-learning models as such. The only hooks an AI deployment encounters are the ordinary obligations that attach whenever Personal Data is Processed by automated means (section 1(1) of the application provisions confirms the Regulations apply to Processing "wholly or partly by automated means"). So the binding force is real, but its AI reach is confined to Section 20 and the transparency and DPIA provisions that orbit it.

### International alignment

Section 20 is the GDPR Article 22 analogue and the alignment is close to exact. The right in 20(1), the three exceptions in 20(2), the human-intervention / express-a-view / contest safeguards in 20(3), and the special-category restriction in 20(4) track Article 22(1) to (4) almost word for word; the Profiling definition in Section 62 is the Article 4(4) text; and the "meaningful information about the logic involved, as well as the significance and the envisaged consequences" formula in the notice provisions is lifted directly from GDPR Articles 13(2)(f) and 14(2)(g). The one notable local colouring is the explicit naming of "fraud prevention, anti-money laundering and security and integrity purposes" in the Applicable-Law exception at 20(2)(c), which reflects the ADGM's character as a financial centre and gives regulated firms a clear statutory basis for automated AML and fraud screening, subject to the written-notice and one-month-reconsideration conditions. For a firm already mapped to GDPR Article 22, ADGM Section 20 requires essentially no incremental conceptual work.

### Coverage and scope

The Regulations govern AI only indirectly, through the single, narrow lens of solely-automated decisions on Personal Data, and the limits should be stated plainly. There is no concept of "high-risk AI," no AI or algorithm register, no certification or audit regime, no design principles for autonomous systems, and no Autonomous Systems Officer. Section 20 bites only where three conditions coincide: a decision is based **solely** on automated Processing, it concerns **Personal Data**, and it produces **legal or similarly significant effects**. AI that informs rather than determines an outcome, that processes no Personal Data, or that has no legally significant effect falls outside Section 20 entirely. The Section 34 DPIA is the only structured risk instrument, and it is a generic data-protection tool keyed to "high risk to the rights of natural persons," not an AI conformity assessment. The contrast with DIFC Regulation 10 is stark and worth drawing explicitly: where the DIFC built a bespoke binding layer over its data-protection law (Deployer/Operator/Provider roles, transparency notices, human-intervention algorithms, an AI register, design principles, a high-risk certification gate, and an ASO modelled on the DPO), the ADGM has done none of this. The ADGM relies entirely on the inherited GDPR Article 22 machinery. Two financial free zones in the same country have therefore taken materially different paths: DIFC has a direct, AI-specific binding rule; ADGM has only the indirect, generic data-protection overlay.

### Feasibility and practical effect

For firms, the practical takeaway is straightforward and consequential: a human-in-the-loop advice or decision model sidesteps Section 20 entirely. Because the right in 20(1) is triggered only by decisions based **solely** on automated Processing, retaining a genuine, substantive human decision-maker (not a rubber stamp) takes the decision outside the prohibition, and with it the notice, reconsideration, and safeguarding obligations of 20(2) and 20(3). This is the same well-understood escape route that exists under GDPR Article 22, and it is why much regulated-sector AI in practice is positioned as decision-support rather than decision-making. Where solely-automated decisions are genuinely needed (for example automated AML or fraud screening), the 20(2)(c) Applicable-Law route is available, conditioned on written notice and the one-month reconsideration window. The transparency duty (disclosing the existence of automated decision-making and "meaningful information about the logic involved") and the Section 34 DPIA are the two obligations a firm cannot design around merely by inserting a human, but both are familiar GDPR-style compliance steps rather than novel AI controls.

### Gaps

Assessed as an AI instrument, the Regulations have the same structural gaps as GDPR Article 22 itself, and no more is added to close them. "Solely" and "similarly significantly affects" are left undefined and will require interpretation; the contour of "meaningful information about the logic involved" is unspecified and does not amount to a duty of full algorithmic explainability; and there is no treatment of generative or foundation models, agentic systems, model provenance, training-data governance, or AI-specific bias auditing. The DPIA list of triggering Processing operations is delegated to Commissioner publication (34(4)), so the practical scope of mandatory impact assessment depends on guidance outside the Regulations. These are not defects of the data-protection regime, which is coherent on its own terms; they are simply the boundaries of treating AI governance as a by-product of data-protection law.

### Internal consistency

Within its own frame the instrument is internally consistent. Section 20 anchors the right; the notice provisions in sections 11 to 13 cross-reference 20(1) and 20(4) so the transparency duty and the substantive right are wired together; the special-category restriction in 20(4) ties back to the Section 7 conditions for processing sensitive data; and the Section 34 DPIA provides a general high-risk assessment gate that an AI deployment would pass through alongside any other high-risk Processing. The vocabulary and obligations are imported wholesale from the GDPR, so there is no internal tension of the kind that can arise when a jurisdiction grafts a novel AI layer onto an older statute.

---

## Overall assessment

The ADGM Data Protection Regulations 2021 are binding, well-drafted, GDPR-modelled data-protection law, but they are not an AI regime, and their AI footprint reduces to GDPR Article 22 transposed almost verbatim: a right against solely-automated significant decisions (Section 20), three exceptions including an AML/fraud carve-out (20(2)), human-intervention safeguards (20(3)), a special-category restriction (20(4)), the standard Profiling definition (Section 62), an Article 13/14-style duty to disclose automated decision-making and the logic involved, and a generic Section 34 DPIA. The defining feature of the ADGM position is what is absent: unlike the DIFC, the ADGM has built no AI-specific binding layer, with no high-risk AI category, no AI register, no certification, and no Autonomous Systems Officer. This is consistent with the ADGM's broader technology-neutral posture, where the Financial Services Regulatory Authority has issued no binding AI rulebook and regulates AI through existing, conduct-based and outcome-based rules. The net effect for firms is that AI governance in the ADGM is achieved indirectly, through data-protection compliance, and that a genuine human-in-the-loop model removes most AI deployments from the one provision (Section 20) that would otherwise bite.
