# CBUAE Guidance Note on the Responsible Adoption and Use of AI and ML by Licensed Financial Institutions

*Research and comparison only, not legal advice. AI-assisted analysis; may contain errors, so verify all claims against the primary source document.*

**Document:** Guidance Note on Consumer Protection and the Responsible Adoption and Use of Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning by Licensed Financial Institutions in the U.A.E.
**Issued:** February 2026 (source Word document dated 23 December 2025; PDF rendered 10 March 2026 per file metadata; the guidance text itself is undated)
**Issuing body:** Central Bank of the United Arab Emirates (CBUAE)
**Legal status:** Non-binding supervisory guidance; expressed as "should" expectations; no penalties or standalone enforcement mechanism, but it supplements existing binding CBUAE regulations and standards
**Scope:** Onshore CBUAE-licensed financial institutions ("LFIs"), expressly including insurance providers; ~7 pages

---

## Summary

This is a short, principles-based guidance note from the Central Bank of the UAE setting out how onshore licensed financial institutions, insurers included, should adopt and use AI and ML in ways that protect consumers and support good market conduct. It is explicitly framed around consumer protection, "with a focus on the end user," and addresses transparency, bias, ethics, accountability, explainability and data privacy. It is non-binding (every operative sentence is a "should") but supervisory: it tells LFIs to develop their own internal AI policies by reference to this Guidance and to follow the CBUAE Model Management Standards, and it is the benchmark against which a CBUAE-supervised firm's AI governance will be assessed.

## Key points

The note is organised into ten numbered sections, preceded by an introduction that ties it to the UAE Charter for the Development & Use of AI (July 2024), the UAE National Strategy for AI, and the CBUAE Guidelines for Financial Institutions adopting Enabling Technologies. It stresses the principles "should not be taken to be static or fixed."

1. **Definitions.** Defines **AI** (machine-based systems performing tasks normally requiring human intelligence, with varying autonomy and the ability to learn and adapt), **GenAI** (models that generate human-like text, audio and images, including Large Language Models), and **ML** (a subset of AI learning from data without explicit programming). The organising concept is the **"high-impact decision"**: any determination by an LFI using AI that "materially affects a customer's access to financial products or services," for example a loan application or insurance claim. It also defines **MMS** as the Central Bank's **Model Management Standards**.
2. **Governance and Accountability.** LFIs should adopt a documented governance framework commensurate with the size, nature and complexity of operations, following the principles of the MMS. The Board and senior management are accountable for AI systems and outcomes; LFIs "should not employ AI models that they have no control over." Regular performance and risk reporting to senior management and Boards is expected, with defined roles for Audit and Risk Committee, Risk Management, Internal Audit and IT.
3. **Fairness / Non-Discrimination and Ethics.** AI should not produce discriminatory or manipulative outcomes, and should not be deployed if it is or becomes such post-deployment. Training data should be accurate, relevant and representative of the customer populations. AI should be subject to periodic testing, "i.e. once a year or each time a model is upgraded, materially changed or a new one is introduced," to identify and remediate embedded bias.
4. **Transparency and Explainability.** LFIs should be transparent about AI use, "particularly in respect of high-impact decisions," and disclose when a customer is interacting with an AI application. Disclosures should be "plain language and accurate... in both **Arabic and English**, with telephone support available in all major languages of the UAE." LFIs should consider providing **opt-out rights**, particularly for high-impact decisions.
5. **Data Quality, Privacy and Security.** Models should use accurate, relevant, up-to-date data with clear provenance and audit trails, in compliance with the UAE Personal Data Protection Law and the UAE Information Assurance Regulation. The note invokes the Consumer Protection Standards and **"rules on data being retained in the country"** (a data-residency point). It calls for privacy-by-design and security-by-design, stress testing, operational resilience, and use of AI to detect fraud and AML disparities.
6. **Continuous Monitoring and Review.** Per the MMS, AI should be subject to continuous monitoring; LFIs should consistently monitor and, where appropriate, update or cease using models. Independent third-party experts "willing to challenge and question" AI use should be engaged periodically. LFIs should retain "the clear and immediate ability, with human intervention, to cease use of an AI model."
7. **Human Oversight and Consumer Protection.** AI should operate under "meaningful human oversight and judgement," particularly for decisions with significant consumer implications. The note sets out three oversight models: **(i) Human-in-the-loop** (AI recommends, a human retains full authority to approve or reject); **(ii) Human-on-the-loop** (AI works autonomously on routine tasks, a human monitors and can intervene); and **(iii) Human-out-of-the-loop** (AI operates without direct human involvement, only for low-risk, non-material processes with appropriate controls). The level of human involvement should be commensurate with the risk to the consumer. Consumers should be able to request human review or explanation, be offered alternative arrangements where they do not wish to be subject to an AI decision, and have access to complaints and redress channels in line with Article 8 of the Consumer Protection Regulation.
8. **Integration with Existing Frameworks.** AI tools should be integrated into the enterprise-wide risk management framework; AI risk assessments "should not operate in isolation." Policies should complement rather than duplicate the Consumer Protection Regulation and other CBUAE directives, with consumer risk treated as part of the conduct risk framework.
9. **Outsourcing and Third-Party Risk.** Per section 4.7 of the MMS and the Outsourcing Regulation for Banks, due diligence on AI vendors and cloud providers is expected, with contractual audit and information rights. Procurement should be documented, include annual cybersecurity reviews and pre-deployment testing, and LFIs should maintain an inventory of AI models (including third-party-hosted ones), holding third-party models to the same fairness, explainability and robustness standards. LFIs should avoid over-reliance on a single AI provider.
10. **Ethical Collaboration and Innovation.** LFIs are encouraged to collaborate with peers, academia, the CBUAE and to participate in the UAE AI sandboxes and Innovation Hub, and to publish case studies (which may be anonymised). The note closes by confirming it shall supplement, not replace, applicable laws and CBUAE directives.

## Observations

### Legal status

This is the decisive interpretive point. The note is non-binding guidance, not a regulation: it uses "should" throughout, "encourages," and expressly states that it "shall supplement and not replace any laws, regulations or directives issued by the CBUAE or other competent authorities." There are no penalties, no licensing condition, and no standalone compliance process attached to the note itself. That said, it is supervisory soft law, not an ethics charter. The CBUAE is the prudential and conduct regulator for the firms it addresses, and the note repeatedly tethers itself to binding instruments: the Model Management Standards, the Consumer Protection Regulation (including Article 8 on redress), the Outsourcing Regulation for Banks, the Personal Data Protection Law and the Information Assurance Regulation. In practice it sets the supervisory benchmark a CBUAE-supervised firm will be measured against in examinations, much as FINMA guidance frames Swiss supervisory expectations without being a statute. The consumer-protection framing is doing real work: it routes AI conduct into an existing, enforceable conduct-risk regime rather than leaving it free-floating.

### Scope

Coverage is the onshore UAE financial sector. "LFIs" are Central Bank licensees, and the note is unusually explicit that this "includ[es] insurance providers," reflecting the CBUAE's post-2020 absorption of the insurance regulator. Critically, it does not reach the two financial free zones: firms regulated in the DIFC (by the DFSA) and the ADGM (by the FSRA) sit outside CBUAE jurisdiction and are governed by their own regimes. This makes the note the onshore counterpart to, and separate from, DIFC Regulation 10 and the ADGM approach, leaving the UAE with parallel financial-sector AI regimes by jurisdiction. A cross-zone firm must therefore map which of its entities this Guidance binds.

### Specificity and clarity

The note is more directive than the UAE Charter but remains qualitative. Its sharpest contribution is the **"high-impact decision"** concept, which is the organising idea: by defining the trigger as any AI determination that "materially affects a customer's access to financial products or services" (loan applications, insurance claims), it concentrates the heaviest expectations (transparency, opt-out, human oversight) on the decisions that matter most to consumers. This is more structured than the FCA's deliberately deferential, technology-neutral, outcomes-based posture, which largely declines to write AI-specific rules and leans on existing principles such as Consumer Duty. It is less elaborated than MAS's FEAT principles, which break Fairness, Ethics, Accountability and Transparency into detailed, assessable sub-principles with a companion assessment methodology (Veritas). The CBUAE note names the right themes and gives a usable risk trigger, but it stops short of FEAT-style operational detail.

### International alignment

The substance is squarely within the international mainstream. The governance, fairness, transparency and human-oversight pillars mirror the OECD AI Principles, MAS FEAT and FINMA's supervisory expectations. Most striking is Section 7's **human-in / human-on / human-out-of-the-loop** trichotomy, which is near-verbatim the terminology used in Singapore's Model AI Governance Framework and echoed in EU AI Act discourse; the CBUAE has imported a recognised taxonomy rather than inventing one. The data-residency expectation and the bilingual Arabic-and-English disclosure duty are the distinctively local features, the latter giving the transparency principle a concrete, jurisdiction-specific shape that most peer frameworks lack.

### Measurability

Most expectations are qualitative and self-assessed ("meaningful human oversight," "sufficient quality," "commensurate with... risks"), which limits testability. There are a few concrete cadences that stand out against that backdrop: bias and discrimination testing "at least once a year or each time a model is upgraded, materially changed or a new one is introduced" (Section 3); annual cybersecurity reviews of third-party providers and pre-deployment testing (Section 9); and a required, maintained inventory of AI models including third-party-hosted ones (Section 9). The annual bias-testing floor is one of the few hard numbers and is a genuinely useful, auditable benchmark. Beyond these, the note relies on the MMS and the firm's own risk appetite to supply quantitative thresholds.

### Gaps

For a supervisory instrument, several omissions are worth flagging. There are no penalties or enforcement consequences specified in the note itself (by design, since enforcement runs through the underlying regulations). There are no quantitative thresholds defining what makes a decision "high-impact" beyond the materiality test, leaving classification to firm judgement. There is no AI-specific incident-reporting timeline: Section 5 points to existing legal and regulatory reporting requirements, and Section 6 calls for mechanisms to "detect, report and remediate," but no defined window or channel for notifying the CBUAE of an AI failure or consumer-harm event. The note also leans heavily on documents it does not reproduce, principally the MMS, so its real bite depends on the rigour of those standards. GenAI is defined but barely governed as a distinct category: there are no specific provisions on model provenance for foundation models, hallucination risk, or generative-content disclosure beyond the general transparency duty.

## Overall assessment

The CBUAE Guidance Note is a competent, consumer-protection-centred supervisory framework that brings the UAE's onshore financial sector into line with international practice on AI governance. Its strengths are a clear organising concept (the "high-impact decision"), a recognised and well-chosen human-oversight taxonomy, a concrete annual bias-testing cadence, a model inventory requirement, and a deliberate integration with the existing MMS, Consumer Protection, Outsourcing and data-protection regimes rather than the creation of a parallel silo. Its limits are those of guidance: it is non-binding, qualitative in most expectations, silent on penalties and on any AI-specific incident-reporting timeline, and dependent on instruments it only references. It should be read not as a standalone rulebook but as the supervisory benchmark that tells CBUAE-licensed firms, insurers included, how the Central Bank expects them to operationalise responsible AI, with enforcement flowing through the binding conduct and prudential framework into which it is consciously woven. It is best understood as the onshore complement to the DIFC and ADGM regimes, comparable in posture to FINMA guidance and thematically aligned with MAS FEAT and the OECD, and a meaningful step up in specificity from the aspirational UAE Charter on which it builds.
