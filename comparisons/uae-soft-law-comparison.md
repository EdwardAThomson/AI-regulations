# The UAE and the Soft-Law AI Jurisdictions: UK, Singapore, Switzerland, UAE

> Deep-dive. For the top-level map of the whole soft-law field (including the USA), start at [Soft-Law AI Jurisdictions](soft-law-ai-jurisdictions.md).

The UAE belongs to the group of jurisdictions that have **not** enacted a comprehensive,
horizontal AI law and instead govern through strategy, voluntary frameworks, and sector
regulators. On that axis it sits alongside the UK, Singapore and Switzerland (see the
[small open economies comparison](small-open-economies-comparison.md)). But the UAE breaks the
pattern of that trio in two structurally important ways, and this comparison foregrounds them:

1. The UAE has a genuinely **binding, AI-specific** obligation in its financial free zones
   (DIFC Regulation 10). The UK, Singapore and Switzerland have **no** binding AI-specific rules.
2. For the UAE, the **EU AI Act is not the de-facto compliance floor.** The defining external
   anchor for UK/Singapore/Switzerland firms does not anchor the UAE the same way.

Based on the [UAE analyses](../UAE/README.md), the three trio jurisdictions' reviews, and the
[small open economies comparison](small-open-economies-comparison.md).

---

## The shared pattern (why the UAE is in this group)

1. **No comprehensive AI Act.** The UAE, like the trio, has no horizontal binding AI statute.
   Federal posture is a national strategy plus a voluntary ethics charter.
2. **Voluntary and/or sector-regulator-led.** Governance runs through frameworks (UAE Charter,
   Dubai ethics guidelines), sector regulators (CBUAE, SCA, DFSA, FSRA), and generally
   applicable law (PDPL).
3. **Pro-innovation framing.** The UAE positions light-touch regulation plus aggressive state
   adoption as a competitive advantage, the same rhetorical move as the trio.
4. **Principle-based financial-sector guidance.** The CBUAE's 2026 AI/ML guidance is the same
   "principles, proportionate, technology-neutral" species as FCA/MAS/FINMA.

## Where the UAE breaks the trio's pattern

### 1. It has binding AI-specific obligations (the others do not)

This is the single biggest divergence. The trio's defining feature is that **no firm faces a
binding AI-specific obligation under domestic law**: governance is voluntary or rides on
existing regulators' general powers. The UAE is different: a firm established in the **DIFC** that
processes personal data through autonomous or semi-autonomous systems is subject to **DIFC
Regulation 10**, which imposes binding duties (transparency notices, an AI register,
human-intervention mechanisms, and for high-risk processing both certification and an appointed
**Autonomous Systems Officer**). Regulation 10 borrows EU AI Act concepts (deployer/operator,
risk and impact assessment, human oversight) but lodges them in a data-protection instrument
rather than a product-safety one.

So the accurate description of the UAE is **"soft-law federally, hard-edged in the free zones."**
It is the only jurisdiction in this group where "which sub-jurisdiction is the firm in?" changes
whether binding AI rules apply at all.

### 2. The EU AI Act is not its compliance floor

For the UK, Singapore and Switzerland, the EU AI Act is the structural reality: their firms serving
EU users must comply regardless of domestic law, so domestic frameworks are additive on top of an
EU floor. Switzerland additionally faces treaty-adjacent equivalence pressure.

The UAE does not sit on that EU floor in the same way. Its economy and its AI firms are oriented to
the Gulf, wider MENA, Asia and the US (the Stargate UAE and US-UAE compute partnerships are
illustrative). EU market access matters for some UAE firms, but it is not the gravitational anchor
of UAE AI governance. Tellingly, the external reference points named in DIFC Regulation 10 are
**OECD, UNESCO and the NIST AI framework** rather than the EU AI Act as a compliance baseline, and
the UAE is **not** a signatory of the Council of Europe AI Convention that anchors Switzerland.
This makes the UAE more genuinely autonomous in its governance choices than the trio, whose
"distinctiveness" is bounded by the EU floor.

### 3. State-as-adopter, not just light regulator

The trio are, in posture, **regulators and conveners** that have chosen restraint. The UAE is an
**aggressive deployer**. It created the world's first Minister of State for AI in 2017, has
appointed Chief AI Officers across government, plans to seat a National AI System as an advisory
member of Cabinet and federal boards from January 2026, and in 2025 stood up a Regulatory
Intelligence Office to use AI in drafting and revising legislation. No trio jurisdiction embeds AI
into the machinery of the state to this degree. The UAE's "pro-innovation" claim is therefore
backed by adoption and industrial policy (Falcon, MBZUAI, G42, sovereign compute), not merely by
the absence of regulation, which is the dimension on which Switzerland's claim is thinnest.

### 4. Political form changes the tempo

The trio govern through legislatures, consultations and (for the UK and Switzerland) democratic
accountability that slows and constrains change. The UAE governs by **decree and Cabinet
decision**, and its free zones legislate independently. This lets the UAE issue, pivot, and
embed AI initiatives quickly (an AI minister in 2017; a binding free-zone AI rule in 2023; an
AI Cabinet advisor announced for 2026), but it also means governance is less the product of
public deliberation and more an instrument of executive strategy.

## Financial-sector AI: the cleanest apples-to-apples

All four have financial-sector AI guidance, and here the UAE's hybrid character is sharpest.

| | UK FCA | Singapore MAS | Switzerland FINMA | UAE |
|--|--------|---------------|-------------------|-----|
| Instrument | AI Update (2024) | FEAT (2018) + Veritas | Guidance 08/2024 | CBUAE AI/ML Guidance (2026); DIFC Reg 10; ADGM DP; joint 2021 guidelines |
| Binding? | No | No | No (supervisory weight) | **Onshore CBUAE: no (guidance). DIFC: yes (Reg 10).** |
| Character | Maps 5 principles onto existing rules | 4 principles + testing toolkit | 7 supervisory assessment areas | CBUAE: 10-section consumer-protection guidance; DIFC: binding data-protection AI rule |

The CBUAE's 2026 guidance shares the trio's DNA (principle-based, proportionate,
technology-neutral, anchored in existing frameworks; it even tells institutions to follow the
Model Management Standards). Its organising idea is the **"high-impact decision"** (a
determination materially affecting a customer's access to a financial product, e.g. a loan or an
insurance claim), with heavier expectations the higher the impact. That is recognisably the same
family as FINMA's materiality-based assessment and MAS's FEAT.

But the UAE is the only one of the four where a financial firm can face a **binding** AI-adjacent
obligation: a DIFC-licensed firm using AI on personal data is inside Regulation 10. A firm should
therefore first ask **where it is licensed** (onshore vs DIFC vs ADGM) before asking what its AI
obligations are. See the [UAE financial-services analysis](../UAE/analysis/financial-services-ai-governance.md).

## Where the UAE sits on the spectrum

If the trio occupy a band of "soft-law, EU-floored, restraint-as-strategy," the UAE is adjacent
but distinct:

- **Softer** than the trio at the federal level (a voluntary 12-principle charter is lighter than
  the UK's articulated 5-principle regime or Singapore's layered framework stack), **but**
- **Harder** than any of them in its free zones (a binding AI rule none of the trio has), **and**
- **More autonomous** (not bound to the EU floor), **and**
- **More interventionist as a state** (adoption and industrial policy rather than convening).

The honest summary: the UAE is not a smaller copy of the UK or Singapore. It is a **pro-adoption,
free-zone-fragmented, industrial-policy state** that happens to share the soft-law label. The
shared label is real, but the governance logic underneath is different: the trio are managing
restraint against an EU floor; the UAE is building an AI economy under direct state direction,
with hard rules switched on selectively where it has chosen common-law free zones.

---

## Practical implications

1. **Do not assume "soft-law" means "no binding AI rules" in the UAE.** It does federally; it does
   not in the DIFC. Check the licensing sub-jurisdiction first.
2. **Do not assume the EU AI Act is the UAE baseline.** It is for trio firms; for a UAE firm with
   no EU exposure, the relevant stack is domestic (PDPL, free-zone DP law, sector guidance).
3. **The trio's distinctive institutions are about safety/testing/convening; the UAE's are about
   adoption and sovereign capability.** Engage AISI/AI Verify for the trio; for the UAE, the
   relevant counterparts are the AI Office, Abu Dhabi's AIATC, and the free-zone regulators.
4. **Watch the free zones, not just the federal centre,** for the UAE's binding developments
   (DIFC Regulation 10 full enforcement, January 2026; any ADGM follow-on).
